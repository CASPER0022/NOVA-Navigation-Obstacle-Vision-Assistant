Start here

What NOVA is in one minute, and how to use this notebook.

NOVA is a program that runs on a laptop with a webcam (or on a recorded video). It watches the path in front of a visually impaired person and tells them, by voice and short beeps, what is in their way, where it is, how far away it is, and whether it is coming closer.

A typical moment looks like this. The camera sees a chair. NOVA works out that the chair is about 20° to the right and about 2 meters away. It turns that into words a blind person is trained to understand, "Chair at 1 o'clock, 2 meters", plays a short tone in the right ear first so the user knows which side to react to, and then says the phrase. If the chair is directly in the walking path and very close, NOVA instead says "Stop. Chair ahead, 1 meter" and interrupts anything else it was saying.

The one-sentence pitch

NOVA turns a single ordinary camera into a talking obstacle detector: YOLOv8 finds objects, pinhole-camera geometry turns pixels into meters and clock directions, a tracker measures what is approaching, and a safety-first alert policy decides the one most important thing to say.

What makes it more than "YOLO plus text-to-speech"

Anyone can draw boxes with YOLO and read out the labels. That version is unusable for a blind person: it repeats itself many times per second, says "person" for someone harmlessly standing far to the side, and stays silent when the camera is covered. Most of NOVA's work goes into what happens after detection:

  • Real units. Distances in meters (or steps or feet), directions as true clock positions where 1 hour = 30°.
  • Walking-corridor awareness. Only things that overlap a 1 m wide strip in front of you count as "in your path".
  • Motion. A tracker follows each object over time, so NOVA knows its closing speed and time-to-contact.
  • Three urgency levels (critical, warning, info) and strict rules about what to say, when, and how often.
  • Honesty about its own blindness. It warns when the image is too dark, blurry, blocked or frozen.
  • A non-visual interface. Stereo-panned tones, interruptible speech and keyboard commands that work without looking at the screen.

Safety position (say this early in the presentation)

NOVA is a research and educational aid. It is not a replacement for a white cane, a guide dog or orientation & mobility training, and it must never be the only basis for crossing a road. Distances from a single camera are approximate, and the detector can miss objects it was never trained on, such as poles, kerbs, stairs and holes.

Explain your project in thirty seconds.

NOVA is a real-time assistant for visually impaired people. A camera looks at the path ahead. YOLOv8 detects objects, we convert each box into a real distance in meters and a clock direction using the pinhole camera model, a tracker measures whether things are approaching, and a rule-based alert policy speaks the single most urgent hazard, for example "Stop. Chair ahead, 1 meter", with a stereo tone that tells you the side before the words finish.

What is the main contribution, given that YOLO already exists?

YOLO only gives boxes and labels. Our contribution is the layer that makes those boxes useful and safe for a blind user: metric distance and clock direction from geometry, the walking-corridor test, tracking with closing speed and time-to-contact, a three-level urgency model with announce-once rules so the user is not flooded, camera-health self-checks, and an audio interface designed for non-visual use. We also built the evaluation tooling (metrics logging and analysis) and a 63-case test suite.

Who is the target user and what would they actually carry?

A blind or low-vision pedestrian who already uses a cane or guide dog. The prototype runs on a laptop with a webcam, which is fine for a demo. A real product would run the same pipeline on a phone or a small chest- or head-mounted camera with bone-conduction headphones, so the ears stay open to traffic sounds.

Is it a replacement for the white cane?

No, and we say so in the README. The cane reliably finds ground-level obstacles and drop-offs within about 1.5 m. NOVA adds what the cane cannot: objects higher up (a table edge, a person), objects farther away, and things moving towards you. They complement each other.

Does it need the internet?

No. Detection (YOLOv8n), geometry, tracking, alert logic and speech (pyttsx3 using the operating system's built-in voices) all run locally. That keeps latency predictable, works without signal, and means no camera images leave the device, which matters for privacy because the camera films other people in public.

The problem and the research behind it

Why this project matters, and what the literature told us to build.

The scale of the problem

The World Health Organization estimates that at least 2.2 billion people worldwide have a near or distance vision impairment, and that at least 1 billion of those cases could have been prevented or have not yet been addressed. Our reference paper quotes the older WHO 2013 figure of 285 million visually impaired people, of whom 39 million are blind, and notes that about 90% live in developing countries. That last point is one reason we kept NOVA cheap: one ordinary camera, no special sensors, no paid cloud services.

Traditional aids and their limits

AidStrengthLimitation NOVA tries to cover
White caneCheap, reliable, finds ground-level obstacles and drop-offsRange is very short (at most about 1.5 m, per the review paper) and it misses things above waist height
Guide dogExcellent navigation partnerLong, expensive training; limited supply; the owner must care for a living animal
Sighted guideFull understanding of the sceneNot always available; reduces independence
Ultrasonic "smart canes"Detect that something is thereCannot say what it is (a wall, a person, a car) or whether it is moving towards you

Our reference paper

The project is based on "A review of assistive spatial orientation and navigation technologies for the visually impaired" by Fernandes, Costa, Filipe, Paredes and Barroso (Universal Access in the Information Society, 2019; DOI 10.1007/s10209-017-0570-8). It is an umbrella review: it surveys many systems instead of proposing one. It splits an assistive navigation system into four modules: location, orientation (how you relate to things around you), navigation (route to a destination) and interface (how information reaches the user).

NOVA deliberately focuses on orientation and interface, the "what is around me right now and is it dangerous?" part. Location (GPS, indoor positioning) and route navigation are out of scope.

Panel questions

6 questions
Why did you choose a camera instead of ultrasonic or LiDAR sensors?

An ultrasonic sensor only says "something is X cm away". A camera can say what it is (a person, a car, a chair), and that changes what the user should do: a person will move out of the way, a car is dangerous, a chair must be walked around. Cameras are also cheap and already in every phone. LiDAR and depth cameras give better distance but are more expensive and not in most phones. The trade-off is that our distances are estimates. We discuss depth sensing as future work.

Your reference paper is a review from 2017–2019. Isn't it outdated?

Its technology survey is dated, but we used it for the parts that do not age: what blind users need (orientation, filtered information, clock directions, danger overriding everything) and how to structure such a system. For the vision technology itself we used current tools: YOLOv8 from 2023.

Why did you drop the "Meta API" text-to-speech from your original diagram?

Three reasons. Latency: a network round trip adds hundreds of milliseconds and is unpredictable, while a "Stop" warning must come out immediately. Availability: outdoors the network can drop. Privacy and cost: we did not want to send data to a third-party service. Offline pyttsx3 uses the voices built into Windows, starts speaking almost at once and costs nothing.

Which of the paper's four modules does NOVA implement?

Mainly orientation (what is around the user, where and how far) and interface (speech, tones, keys). We do not do location (GPS or indoor positioning) or route navigation. That was a scoping decision: obstacle awareness is the safety-critical part and is a pure computer vision problem, which fits a CV course.

How is NOVA different from apps like Microsoft Seeing AI or Google Lookout?

Those apps are strong at on-demand description: reading text, recognising products, describing a scene when you point the phone. NOVA is built around continuous, safety-oriented monitoring: it decides by itself when to interrupt you, ranks hazards by urgency, measures approach speed and time-to-contact, and stays quiet when nothing matters. It also exposes its whole decision logic, which those closed apps do not.

Did you talk to any visually impaired users?

Not formally. Our requirements came from the review paper and from orientation & mobility conventions such as clock directions. A user study with blind participants is the most important next step before any real-world claim, and we list it under future work. Answer this honestly if asked.

NOVA at a glance: the pipeline

The path one camera frame takes, from pixels to a spoken warning.

NOVA is a pipeline: each stage takes the previous stage's output, adds information, and passes it on. Learn this sequence first, because every later chapter zooms into one stage.

Capture a framenova/capture.py

Read an image from the webcam, a video file or a folder of images. If the camera disconnects, try to reconnect for up to 30 seconds while telling the user.

Detect objects with YOLOv8nova/perception.py

The neural network returns boxes, labels and confidence scores. We keep only 26 object types that matter for walking.

Add geometrynova/perception.py

Each box becomes a Detection with an angle, a clock hour, a distance in meters, and an "in my walking path?" flag.

Track over timenova/tracker.py

Match today's boxes with the boxes from a moment ago, so each object keeps one ID. Smooth its distance and measure its closing speed.

Check the camera and traffic lightsnova/environment.pynova/traffic_light.py

Is the image too dark, blurry, blocked or frozen? What colour is the nearest traffic light?

Decide what to saynova/alerts.py

Rank every object as critical, warning or info, apply the "say it once" rules, and produce at most one announcement.

Speak itnova/audio.pynova/phrasing.py

Play a stereo tone that encodes urgency and direction, then speak the phrase. Critical messages interrupt.

Show it (optional)nova/hud.pynova/radar.pynova/captions.py

Draw boxes, the bird's-eye radar and captions for sighted helpers and demos; optionally record to video.

The conductor that runs these stages in a loop is nova/app.py, started by assistive_system.py.

Fast loop, slower brain

The main loop reads and displays frames as fast as the camera delivers them (about 20–30 per second). But the expensive detection step runs only every inference_interval = 0.25 seconds on a live camera, that is, at most 4 times per second. This is called decoupling display from inference: the preview stays smooth and the CPU is not overloaded, while 4 checks per second is plenty for walking speed (a person walking at 1.4 m/s moves only 35 cm between checks).

Four threads working together

A thread is a separate line of execution inside one program. NOVA uses four so that slow things never block fast things:

ThreadWhat it doesWhy it is separate
Main loopCapture, detect, track, decide, drawThe core pipeline
Speech workerSpeaks one phrase at a timeSpeaking a sentence takes 1–3 s; the camera must keep running meanwhile
Earcon workerPlays the short tonesPlaying audio blocks; a tone must not delay detection
Keyboard listenerReads keys from the terminalWaiting for a key press must not freeze the loop

The "dual-path" safety idea

Our roadmap describes a fast path and a slow path. Safety alerts always go through the fast path: simple rules and numbers that take microseconds after detection. Anything slow, such as a future language-model scene description, may only run on the slow path, on request, and can never delay a "Stop". This is why the alert policy is hand-written rules and not a learned model.

Panel questions

5 questions
Why only 4 detections per second? Wouldn't more be safer?

At walking speed (about 1.4 m/s) you move 35 cm in 0.25 s, so a fresh check every 0.25 s is enough to warn in time. Running YOLO on every frame would use the CPU fully, make the laptop hot and drain the battery, without noticeably improving warnings. The interval is configurable (--inference-interval 0 runs every frame) if a faster computer is available. Recorded videos are always analysed frame by frame so results are repeatable.

Why is the system a pipeline of separate modules instead of one script?

The first version was one long script with global variables, and it had real bugs, including a merge-conflict marker that stopped it from starting. Splitting it into modules (capture, perception, tracker, alerts, audio and so on) means each piece has one job, can be tested on its own without a camera, and can be replaced later (for example a different detector or TTS engine) without touching the rest.

Why use threads? Isn't Python's GIL a problem?

The GIL stops two threads running Python code at the same time, but our extra threads mostly wait: for the speech engine, for audio playback, for a key press. Waiting releases the GIL, so threads are the right tool. The heavy work (YOLO inference) runs inside PyTorch, which also releases the GIL while it computes.

What happens if detection takes longer than 0.25 s?

Nothing breaks. The loop simply runs the detector again as soon as the interval has passed, so on a slow machine NOVA just checks less often. Our logs measured a median of about 72 ms per detection on the test laptop, well under 0.25 s.

Why are safety alerts rule-based and not learned with machine learning?

Three reasons. Predictability: we can say exactly why the system said "Stop" and test it. Speed: rules take microseconds. Data: training a policy would need labelled recordings of what a blind user should be told, which does not exist. The learned part (YOLO) does what learning is good at, recognising objects; the rules do what rules are good at, consistent safety decisions.

Computer vision foundations

What an image is to a computer, and the basic operations NOVA relies on.

An image is a grid of numbers

To a computer, a colour photo is a 3-dimensional array: height × width × 3. Each cell is a pixel, and each pixel has three numbers from 0 to 255 for the amount of blue, green and red light. A 640×480 webcam frame is therefore 640 × 480 × 3 = 921,600 numbers. In Python we hold it as a NumPy array, so frame.shape is (480, 640, 3): rows (height) first, then columns (width), then channels.

OpenCV quirk to remember

OpenCV stores colours in BGR order (blue, green, red), not RGB. That is why the HUD colours in nova/hud.py look "backwards": (0, 0, 255) is red, (0, 165, 255) is orange.

Pixel coordinates start at the top-left corner: x grows to the right, y grows downwards. A bounding box is written (x1, y1, x2, y2): top-left corner and bottom-right corner.

Colour spaces: BGR, grayscale and HSV

A colour space is a way of describing colour with numbers. NOVA uses three:

  • BGR: what the camera gives us. Good for display, bad for asking "is this red?", because brightness is mixed into all three numbers.
  • Grayscale: one brightness number per pixel, computed as a weighted mix (about 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B, since our eyes are most sensitive to green). Used for the camera-health checks, where colour does not matter.
  • HSV: Hue (which colour, as an angle on a colour wheel), Saturation (how pure or grey it is) and Value (how bright). Because hue is separated from brightness, "is this light red?" becomes a simple range check on one number. In OpenCV, hue runs 0–179 (the 0–360° wheel halved to fit in one byte) and red sits at both ends of the range, around 0 and around 179. We use HSV to read traffic lights (Chapter 10).

Filters and convolution

A convolution slides a small grid of weights (a kernel, often 3×3) over the image. At each position it multiplies the kernel with the pixels underneath and adds them up, producing one output pixel. Different kernels do different things: averaging kernels blur, difference kernels find edges.

The Laplacian kernel measures how quickly brightness changes in all directions (a second derivative). Sharp images have many strong edges, so their Laplacian output varies a lot; blurred images have soft edges, so it varies little. The variance of the Laplacian is therefore a classic one-number sharpness score. NOVA uses it to warn "Camera image is blurry".

Laplacian kernel: [ 0 1 0 ] [ 1 −4 1 ] [ 0 1 0 ] sharpness = variance( Laplacian(gray image) ) Low variance = few edges = blurry or featureless image. NOVA's threshold is 15 on a 160×120 grayscale copy.

From filters to neural networks

Hand-designed filters find simple things like edges. Recognising a "chair" needs thousands of filters combined in clever ways, which nobody can design by hand. A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) learns its filters from labelled examples:

  1. Early layers learn edges and colour blobs.
  2. Middle layers combine those into textures and parts (a wheel, a chair leg, an eye).
  3. Deep layers combine parts into whole objects (a car, a person).

Training works like this: show the network an image, compare its guess with the correct answer using a loss function (a number for "how wrong"), then use backpropagation and gradient descent to nudge every weight slightly in the direction that reduces the loss. Repeat over hundreds of thousands of images. We did not train our own network; we use one already trained on the COCO dataset. This is called using a pre-trained model.

Analogy for teammates

A CNN is like a factory line of inspectors. The first inspectors only notice lines and colours. The next ones notice shapes made of those lines. The last ones say "those shapes together are a bicycle". Training is how each inspector learns what to look for.

5 questions
Why do you convert to HSV for traffic lights instead of checking RGB values?

In RGB, a bright red and a dim red have very different numbers in all three channels, so "is it red?" needs complicated rules. HSV separates hue (which colour) from value (brightness), so red is just "hue near 0 or near 179" no matter how bright it is. We then add minimum saturation and brightness to keep only the glowing, strongly coloured lamp pixels.

Why is OpenCV's hue range 0–179 and not 0–360?

OpenCV stores each channel in one byte (0–255) for 8-bit images. 360 does not fit, so the hue angle is divided by 2. Red, which sits at 0° and 360° on the colour wheel, therefore appears at both 0–10 and 165–179, which is why our red range has two parts.

Why variance of the Laplacian to detect blur?

It is cheap (one 3×3 filter and a variance) and well established for focus measurement. Blur removes sharp edges, and the Laplacian responds strongly only to sharp edges, so its variance drops sharply on blurred images. In our test, a normal frame scored about 1499 and a Gaussian-blurred copy about 5.3, against a threshold of 15.

Why do you shrink the image to 160×120 before the health checks?

Speed and stability. The checks only need overall brightness, contrast and sharpness, which a small image keeps. Shrinking with area averaging also removes sensor noise that could otherwise look like "sharp detail" and hide real blur. The cost is a fraction of a millisecond.

What does "pre-trained" mean, and is using a pre-trained model cheating?

It means the network's weights were already learned on a large dataset (COCO, about 118,000 training images). Using it is standard engineering practice, called transfer of a foundation component. Our work is everything around it: geometry, tracking, decision logic and the interface. We would only retrain or fine-tune if we needed new classes such as stairs or poles, which is listed as future work.

Object detection and YOLOv8

How the network finds objects, how detectors are scored, and why we picked YOLOv8n.

Three different tasks

TaskQuestion it answersOutput
ClassificationWhat is in this image?One label ("dog")
Object detectionWhat objects are here, and where?A list of boxes, each with a label and a confidence
SegmentationWhich exact pixels belong to each object?A mask per object or per pixel

NOVA needs detection: we must know where each object is (for direction and distance) and what it is (to phrase it and judge danger). Segmentation would give slightly better shape information but costs more computation, and a box is enough for our geometry.

Key detection vocabulary

Bounding box and confidence

A box (x1, y1, x2, y2) plus a confidence between 0 and 1: how sure the model is that the box contains that object. NOVA keeps boxes with confidence ≥ 0.40 (setting confidence).

IoU: Intersection over Union

IoU measures how much two boxes overlap: the area they share divided by the area they cover together. 1.0 means identical boxes, 0 means no overlap. It is used everywhere in detection, and NOVA also uses it to match objects between frames (Chapter 7).

IoU(A, B) = area(A ∩ B) / area(A ∪ B) = overlap / (area A + area B − overlap) Example: two 10×10 boxes shifted by 5 px sideways overlap in a 5×10 area = 50. IoU = 50 / (100 + 100 − 50) = 1/3. This exact case is a unit test in tests/test_tracker.py.

NMS: Non-Maximum Suppression

A detector usually proposes several overlapping boxes for the same object. NMS keeps the most confident one and deletes the others that overlap it by more than an IoU threshold. Ultralytics runs NMS for us inside model.predict.

Precision, recall and mAP

  • Precision: of the boxes the model drew, how many were correct? (Few false alarms = high precision.)
  • Recall: of the real objects, how many did the model find? (Few misses = high recall.)
  • Raising the confidence threshold increases precision and lowers recall, and the reverse. The precision–recall curve shows this trade-off; the area under it is Average Precision (AP) for one class.
  • mAP (mean Average Precision) averages AP over all classes. mAP@50-95, the COCO standard, also averages over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95, so boxes must be well placed, not just roughly right.

Two families of detectors

Two-stage detectors (R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN) first propose regions that might contain objects, then classify each region. They are accurate but slower. One-stage detectors (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet) predict boxes and classes in a single pass over the image. YOLO ("You Only Look Once", Redmon et al., 2016) started the one-stage real-time family. NOVA needs real time on a laptop CPU, so a one-stage detector is the natural choice.

Inside YOLOv8

YOLOv8 was released by Ultralytics in 2023. It has three parts:

  1. Backbone: a CNN (built from "C2f" blocks, a refinement of the CSP design) that turns the image into feature maps at several scales.
  2. Neck: a feature pyramid (FPN/PAN style) that mixes fine details from early layers with meaning from deep layers, so both small and large objects are found.
  3. Head: predicts, at each location of each scale, whether an object's centre is there, its class scores and its box edges. YOLOv8's head is anchor-free (it predicts distances to the box edges directly, instead of adjusting predefined "anchor" boxes) and decoupled (separate branches for "what class" and "where exactly"), which the Ultralytics docs describe as a "split" head.

Before the network, the image is resized and padded (letterboxed) to 640 pixels on its longer side. After the network, NMS removes duplicate boxes. Ultralytics does both inside model.predict().

Why the "n" model

YOLOv8 comes in sizes n (nano), s, m, l and x. Official numbers from the Ultralytics documentation (COCO validation, 640 px):

For a safety system, a fresh answer 4 times a second matters more than slightly better boxes, and it must run on an ordinary laptop or, later, a phone. On our test laptop, NOVA's YOLOv8n detection on a 640×480 frame took a median of 44 ms in our benchmark (Chapter 15).

COCO and our 26 classes

The model was trained on COCO (Common Objects in Context, Lin et al., 2014): 80 everyday classes. Many are irrelevant for walking (toothbrush, pizza, kite). In nova/perception.py, OBJECT_CATALOG keeps 26 classes, and we pass them to YOLO (classes=self.class_ids) so it does not even report the others. Panel questions

9 questions
Why YOLO and not Faster R-CNN?

Faster R-CNN is a two-stage detector: accurate, but typically several times slower, and impractical in real time on a laptop CPU. NOVA must react within a fraction of a second on modest hardware, so a one-stage real-time detector is the right fit. YOLOv8 also has a mature Python package (Ultralytics) that handles preprocessing, NMS and model download.

Why YOLOv8 and not SSD, DETR or a newer YOLO version?

SSD is older and less accurate at similar speed. DETR (transformer-based) is elegant but heavier and slower to run on CPU. Newer YOLO versions (v9 to v11) give modest accuracy gains; YOLOv8 was stable, very well documented and widely used when we started, and our code wraps the detector in one small class (Detector), so switching to a newer Ultralytics model is a one-line change of the weights file.

Why the nano model if the bigger ones are more accurate?

Latency and hardware. The nano model has 3.2 M parameters and runs about 3× faster than YOLOv8m on CPU. In a safety system, a warning that arrives on time beats a slightly better box that arrives late. Our measured median is about 44–72 ms per detection, leaving a large margin inside the 250 ms cycle. On a GPU machine, the model can be changed with --model yolov8s.pt.

Why a confidence threshold of 0.40?

It is a balance between missed objects and false alarms. A low threshold (like 0.25, Ultralytics' default) produces more phantom detections, which would mean wrong warnings for the user. A high threshold (like 0.7) misses partially visible or unusual objects. On top of the threshold, our tracker requires an object to be seen on 2 detector cycles before a non-critical alert, which removes most single-frame false positives, so 0.40 can stay moderately permissive. It is a setting (--confidence).

Why only 26 of the 80 COCO classes?

Two reasons. Relevance: a blind pedestrian needs to know about people, vehicles, furniture and street objects, not about "pizza" or "toothbrush". Geometry: our distance estimate needs a typical real size for each class, which only makes sense for objects with fairly consistent sizes. Filtering inside YOLO also saves a little time.

COCO doesn't contain stairs, poles, kerbs or holes. Isn't that a big gap?

Yes, and it is the biggest limitation of NOVA, stated in our safety notice. These are exactly the hazards a cane handles well, which is one reason NOVA is a complement and not a replacement. The fixes are listed as future work: fine-tune YOLO on a navigation dataset that includes these classes, or add monocular depth estimation (Depth Anything, MiDaS) to detect any obstacle or drop-off without needing a class label.

Did you fine-tune or train the model?

No. We use the official COCO-pretrained yolov8n.pt. Fine-tuning would need a labelled dataset of the new classes and evaluation on held-out data. Our contribution is the system around the detector. Fine-tuning is the logical next step for stairs and poles.

What is the difference between mAP@50 and mAP@50-95?

mAP@50 counts a predicted box as correct if its IoU with the true box is at least 0.5, which is loose. mAP@50-95 averages the score over thresholds 0.50, 0.55 … 0.95, so it also rewards precise box placement. YOLOv8n's 37.3 is the stricter mAP@50-95. For NOVA, box precision matters because box size drives our distance estimate.

Why do you warm up the model before saying "ready"?

The first inference of a PyTorch model is much slower than the rest, because it allocates memory and initialises kernels (it can take several seconds on CPU). Detector.warmup() runs one throwaway prediction on a black image, so the first real obstacle is not delayed. Only then does NOVA announce "NOVA ready".

Camera geometry: from pixels to meters and clock hours

The maths in nova/perception.py. This is the most "computer vision" part of NOVA, so know it well.

The pinhole camera model

Imagine a dark box with a tiny hole. Light from every point of an object passes through the hole in a straight line and lands on the back wall, making a small upside-down image. Real cameras use a lens, but mathematically they behave almost exactly like this pinhole camera. Its one important number is the focal length f: the distance from the hole to the image. If we measure it in pixels, we can convert between pixel sizes and real sizes.

Step 1: focal length from the field of view

We usually do not know the focal length in pixels, but we can look up (or measure) the camera's horizontal field of view (HFOV): how wide an angle it sees. Most webcams see 55–80°; NOVA's default is camera_hfov_deg = 60. Half the image width covers half the field of view, so:

f = (W / 2) / tan(HFOV / 2) W = image width in pixels. For a 640-pixel-wide frame and HFOV = 60°: f = 320 / tan(30°) = 320 / 0.5774 = 554.3 px. Code: CameraGeometry.__init__.

Step 2: direction (angle) of an object

An object whose box centre is at pixel column x lies at this horizontal angle from straight ahead:

angle = atan( (x − W/2) / f ) Negative = left, positive = right. Example: x = 480 in a 640-wide frame → atan(160 / 554.3) = atan(0.289) = 16.1° to the right. Code: angle_of(); its inverse x_of_angle() is used to draw sector lines.

Step 3: clock position

Orientation & mobility instructors teach directions as a clock face: 12 o'clock is straight ahead, 3 o'clock is directly right, 9 o'clock directly left. A full circle is 360° over 12 hours, so one hour = 30°.

hour_offset = round(angle / 30°), limited to −3 … +3 clock = 12 + hour_offset (13 → 1, 14 → 2, 15 → 3) 16.1° → round(0.54) = 1 → 1 o'clock. 8° → round(0.27) = 0 → 12 o'clock. Code: clock_position().
A detail examiners like

With a 60° camera, the view only reaches ±30°, so NOVA can only ever say 11, 12 or 1 o'clock. That is correct, not a bug. An earlier version stretched the 60° view across 9-to-3 o'clock and would call something 25° to the right "3 o'clock" (90°), which would send a blind user turning the wrong way. 10 and 2 o'clock need a camera wider than 90°. There is a unit test for this: test_frame_edge_of_60_degree_camera_is_not_3_oclock.

Step 4: distance from known size

Using similar triangles with the object's typical real height H and its box height h in pixels, or its real width and box width:

Z_from_height = f · H_real / h_box Z_from_width = f · W_real / w_box A person (1.70 m) whose box is 314 px tall in a 640-wide, 60° frame: Z = 554.3 × 1.70 / 314 = 3.0 m.

Handling boxes cut off by the frame edge

If a person is so close that their feet are below the bottom of the image, the box is shorter than the real person's image, so the height formula would say "far away", the most dangerous possible mistake. estimate_distance() therefore:

  1. Uses the height estimate only if the box touches neither the top nor the bottom edge (3-pixel margin), and the width estimate only if it touches neither side.
  2. If both are valid, takes the smaller (closer) one. Erring towards "closer" is the safe error: an early warning is better than a late one.
  3. If both are cut off, the object fills the view, so it must be very near: it returns at most 0.8 m.

Step 5: sideways offset and the walking corridor

Knowing the distance Z, we can turn any pixel column into a sideways offset in meters (the same similar triangles, sideways):

lateral(x) = (x − W/2) / f · Z in_path = lateral(x1) < +0.5 m and lateral(x2) > −0.5 m The walking corridor is 1 m wide (corridor_half_width = 0.5), about shoulder width plus a margin. An object is "in your path" if any part of it overlaps that strip. Code: build_detection().

This is why NOVA ignores a person standing 3 m away but 1.5 m to the side: their sideways offset puts them outside the corridor, even though they are fairly close.

A real worked example from our results

On the bus photo (resized to 480×640, HFOV 60°, so f = 240 / tan 30° = 415.7 px), YOLO found a man in a light coat with box (28, 236, 145, 534):

  1. Box size. Width 145 − 28 = 117 px; height 534 − 236 = 298 px. No edge touched, so both estimates are valid.
  2. Distances. From height: 415.7 × 1.70 / 298 = 2.37 m. From width: 415.7 × 0.45 / 117 = 1.60 m. NOVA takes the closer: 1.60 m.
  3. Angle. Centre x = 86.5. atan((86.5 − 240) / 415.7) = atan(−0.369) = −20.3° (left).
  4. Clock. round(−20.3 / 30) = round(−0.68) = −1 → 11 o'clock.
  5. Corridor. Left edge: (28 − 240)/415.7 × 1.6 = −0.82 m. Right edge: (145 − 240)/415.7 × 1.6 = −0.37 m. The right edge is inside −0.5 m, so it overlaps the corridor: in path.
  6. Urgency. In path and closer than 2.5 m → warning.
  7. Speech. 1.6 m is rounded to the nearest half meter: "Person at 11 o'clock, 1.5 meters". This is exactly what NOVA said (Chapter 15, Fig. 15.1).

10 questions
How can you estimate distance with only one camera?

A single image loses depth, so we add prior knowledge: the typical real size of each object class. With the pinhole model, an object's image size is inversely proportional to its distance (Z = f · H / h). Knowing the focal length (from the field of view) and the typical height or width, we solve for distance. It is an estimate: a child is smaller than an adult, so a child looks farther away than they are.

Why not use a stereo camera or a depth sensor?

They give much better distances, but need special hardware: two calibrated cameras, an infrared depth sensor or LiDAR, which most phones and laptops do not have. We wanted NOVA to run on any single webcam. The review paper itself discusses stereo vision and depth cameras as accurate but more demanding. A modern middle path is a monocular depth network (Depth Anything, MiDaS), listed as future work.

How accurate are your distances?

We have not yet measured accuracy against a tape measure; that test (objects at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 m) is planned and described in metrics_plan.md, and we should say so honestly. From the method, the error is roughly proportional to how much an object differs from its typical size: an object 20% taller than typical appears about 20% closer. Box tightness and a wrong field-of-view setting add error. Our real-image results show both a sensible case and a failure case (Chapter 15).

Why do you take the minimum of the width and height estimates?

Safety. Both are uncertain, and the costs of errors are not symmetric: saying an obstacle is farther than it is can cause a collision, while saying it is closer only causes an early warning. Taking the closer estimate builds this asymmetry into the system. The cost is some false alarms, for example a person with outstretched arms gets a wide box and looks too close.

Why use clock positions instead of degrees or left/right?

Clock directions are the standard taught in orientation & mobility training, and our review paper says they are easier to understand than exact degrees and more precise than "a little to the left". "1 o'clock" is short to say, quick to understand and maps to a body movement.

Why 30° per hour? Why not spread the camera's view over the clock?

Because the clock face is a physical convention: 3 o'clock means 90° to your right. If we stretched a 60° view over 9-to-3, "3 o'clock" would really mean 30°, and the user would turn far too much. Being correct matters more than using all 12 hours. This was a real bug in an early version that we fixed.

Why is the corridor 1 meter wide?

Average shoulder width is about 0.45 m; adding a safety margin on each side for arm swing and imprecision gives about 1 m. It is configurable (corridor_half_width). A wider corridor gives more warnings about things you would actually pass safely; a narrower one risks missing something you would clip.

Why is the default field of view 60°, and what happens if it is wrong?

60° is a typical webcam value. If the real HFOV differs, the focal length is wrong, which scales all distances and angles. For example, if the real camera is 70° but we assume 60°, our focal length is too large and distances come out about 20% too long. That is why --hfov is a documented calibration setting and the README asks users to set it. Proper camera calibration (with a checkerboard and OpenCV's calibrateCamera) would give the exact focal length and lens distortion.

What about lens distortion and camera tilt?

We ignore both. Webcams have mild barrel distortion near the edges, and if the camera is tilted down, the horizontal maths is slightly off. For a first prototype at walking distances these errors are small compared with the size assumption. Calibration with OpenCV would remove distortion; an IMU (phone gyroscope) could correct for tilt.

Why does a fully cut-off object get 0.8 m?

If a box touches both the top-bottom and the left-right edges, the object fills the view, and both estimates are only upper bounds (the real object is bigger than what we see, so it is closer than computed). The only safe conclusion is "very near". 0.8 m is below the 1.2 m critical distance, so if it is in the corridor it triggers "Stop". This rule can cause false alarms when a large object such as a bus fills the frame from farther away, which we saw in our results.

Tracking and motion

Giving each object a stable identity, and measuring whether it is coming towards you. Code: nova/tracker.py.

Why tracking is needed

YOLO looks at each frame independently. It does not know that the person in this frame is the same person as 0.25 s ago. Without tracking, NOVA could not:

  • avoid re-announcing the same chair every time it shifts slightly in the image;
  • tell two people standing side by side apart (they would merge into one "person");
  • ignore a one-frame false detection (a "ghost" box that appears once);
  • measure speed, because speed needs the same object's distance at several times.

How matching works

Each tracked object is a Track with an ID, its last box, a hit count, a smoothed distance and a short history of (time, distance) readings. Every detector cycle, Tracker.update():

  1. Computes the IoU between every new detection and every existing track with the same label (a person never matches a chair).
  2. Keeps pairs with IoU ≥ 0.25 and sorts them from best to worst overlap.
  3. Greedy assignment: goes down the sorted list, pairing a detection with a track if neither has been used yet.
  4. Unmatched detections start new tracks with new IDs.
  5. Tracks not seen for more than 1.5 s (max_age) are deleted.

This works because at 4 checks per second, objects move only a little between frames, so the same object's boxes overlap strongly.

Debounce: the hit count

Each time a track is matched, its hits count goes up. The alert policy only gives non-critical alerts for objects with at least 2 hits (MIN_HITS). A phantom box that appears in one frame never reaches 2, so it is never spoken. Critical alerts skip this wait: if something seems to be right in front of you, a possibly-wrong "Stop" is better than a late one.

Smoothing the distance

Box sizes jitter from frame to frame, so raw distances jump around. The tracker smooths them with an exponential moving average (EMA):

smoothed_new = α · measured + (1 − α) · smoothed_old, α = 0.5 Each reading counts half; older readings fade quickly (¼, ⅛ …). Higher α reacts faster but is noisier; lower α is smoother but lags behind a fast approach. 0.5 is a deliberate middle.

Closing speed: least-squares slope

To know if something is approaching, we fit a straight line through the recent (time, distance) points and take its slope. The least-squares slope is the line that minimises the squared vertical errors:

slope = Σ (tᵢ − t̄)(dᵢ − d̄) / Σ (tᵢ − t̄)² closing_speed = −slope (positive = getting closer, in m/s) Uses readings from the last 1.5 s. Needs at least 4 readings spanning at least 0.75 s, otherwise reports 0. One noisy reading cannot fake an approach, because it is averaged with the others.

Why not simply (newest − oldest) / time? Because that uses only two noisy points. Least squares uses all of them, so a single jumpy box barely moves the result. There is a test for exactly this: test_jittery_static_object_is_not_approaching_on_short_history.

Time-to-contact (TTC)

TTC = distance / closing_speed (seconds; infinite if not approaching) A cyclist 6 m away closing at 4 m/s has TTC = 1.5 s, below the 2 s critical_ttc, so NOVA says "Stop" even though 6 m sounds far. A wall 1 m away that is not moving has infinite TTC but is critical anyway because of its distance.

TTC is a well-known idea in perception research: the psychologist David Lee showed in 1976 that humans and animals judge braking from time to collision (called "tau") rather than from distance. Using TTC lets NOVA warn earlier about fast things and later about slow things, which is what a person would do.

The high-frame-rate fix

The history used to be limited to the last 12 readings. At 4 readings per second that is 3 s of history, which is fine. But when every frame is analysed (recorded video, or --inference-interval 0) at about 25 readings per second, 12 readings cover only half a second, below the 0.75 s needed, so closing speed was always 0 and "approaching" never fired. We changed the history to keep readings from the last 2 seconds, however many there are, and added a test (test_closing_speed_at_high_frame_rate). It is a good example of a bug found by testing with recorded video.

8 questions
Why didn't you use DeepSORT or ByteTrack?

We considered them. Both add a Kalman filter and, for DeepSORT, an extra neural network for appearance, which costs CPU time and adds complexity. Our use case has few objects that move slowly relative to our 4 Hz update rate, so box overlap alone matches them reliably. Our tracker is about 100 lines, fully unit-tested and easy to reason about. If we moved to crowded scenes, ByteTrack would be the upgrade (Ultralytics even has it built in).

Why greedy matching instead of the Hungarian algorithm?

The Hungarian algorithm finds the best overall pairing, which matters when several similar boxes overlap ambiguously. With a handful of objects and strong overlaps, the greedy best-first pairing gives the same answer almost always, and it is simpler. Only same-label boxes can match, which removes most ambiguity.

Why an IoU threshold of 0.25?

It must be low enough that a box still matches after the object moves or grows between checks (an approaching object's box grows quickly), and high enough that two neighbouring objects do not steal each other's ID. 0.25 is a common, permissive choice for low frame rates. Tests check that IDs persist while an object moves and that two side-by-side people keep two IDs.

What does the debounce cost in latency?

One detector cycle, about 0.25 s, for non-critical alerts only. Critical alerts are never debounced. Removing single-frame ghosts is worth a quarter of a second for warnings and info, because false alarms make users stop trusting and eventually stop using an assistive device.

How do you compute speed, and why least squares?

We fit a straight line through the distance readings of the last 1.5 s and take its slope; the negative slope is the closing speed. Least squares averages out the jitter of individual box sizes. A two-point difference would let one bad box create a fake "approaching" warning.

Why is the approach threshold 0.35 m/s?

Normal walking speed is about 1.2–1.4 m/s. Noise in monocular distance can produce small apparent speeds even for still objects. 0.35 m/s is well above that noise but well below walking speed, so a person walking towards you counts, while a parked car with jittery boxes does not.

The user is also walking. Doesn't that make everything look like it is approaching?

Yes: closing speed is relative to the camera, so walking towards a parked car makes the car "approach". For collision risk, that is actually the right quantity, since what matters is how fast the gap is shrinking. For phrasing, we only say "approaching" for people, vehicles and animals, so we never say a chair is approaching. Separating the user's own motion from object motion would need ego-motion estimation (for example optical flow or the phone's motion sensors), which is future work.

What happens when YOLO loses an object for a moment, or relabels it?

Two layers handle that. The tracker keeps a track alive for 1.5 s without matches. And the alert policy remembers announced objects for 3 s: if a new track appears overlapping (IoU ≥ 0.3) where an announced object was, even with a different label (a TV that YOLO suddenly calls "laptop"), it inherits the memory and is not announced again.

Deciding what to say: the alert policy

The "brain" of NOVA in nova/alerts.py. It turns many detections into at most one short phrase.

YOLO might report 8 objects 4 times a second. A person can comfortably listen to about one short phrase every second or two, and also needs to hear traffic. So the alert policy must rank, filter and remember. Every detector cycle, AlertPolicy.update() returns either one Announcement or nothing.

Step 1: classify every object's urgency

classify() checks these rules in order and returns the first that matches:

2In the walking corridor and closer than critical_distance (1.2 m)criticalYou are about to walk into it
3Approaching with time-to-contact under critical_ttc (2 s), and in the path or within 15° of straight aheadcriticalCollision course, even if still far
4In the corridor and closer than warning_distance (2.5 m)warningIn your way soon
5Closer than 1.2 m but outside the corridorwarningVery close at your side (an elbow could hit it)

Where the thresholds come from

  • 1.2 m critical: about one and a half walking steps. Walking at 1.4 m/s, it leaves roughly 0.85 s to react, which is about the time to hear "Stop" and halt. It is also just inside the cane's reach of about 1.5 m, where the cane will confirm the obstacle.
  • 2.5 m warning: about 2 seconds of walking: enough time to hear a sentence and change course calmly.
  • 6 m maximum: beyond this, monocular distance errors grow large (a few pixels of box error become meters), and objects that far are rarely relevant to the next steps.
  • 2 s TTC: a common rule of thumb for collision warning; it gives enough time to hear the warning and step aside.

All of these are settings in nova_config.json, validated on load (0 < critical < warning ≤ max).

Step 2: filter by verbosity and debounce

  • Verbosity (V key): minimal says only critical and warning; normal adds info about things in your path and about people, vehicles and animals; detailed says everything.
  • Debounce: non-critical objects need 2 tracker hits (Chapter 7).

Step 3: "say it once" memory

The policy remembers every object it has announced (by track ID): its urgency level, when it was said, and how many times. _due() decides whether an object may speak again:

  • New object: yes.
  • Became more urgent (info → warning → critical): yes, immediately.
  • Same urgency: warnings and info are said once. Critical objects get one reminder after 6 s (critical_repeats = 2 in total), then NOVA stays quiet about them.
  • Less urgent than before: no.

After that, the user can always press Space to hear everything again. This design came from testing: the earlier version repeated "Stop" every few seconds about an object the user already knew about, which was exhausting. The literature calls this alarm fatigue: when alarms are too frequent, people start ignoring them, including the important ones.

Step 4: pick one, respecting gaps

All candidates are sorted by (urgency, distance): most urgent first, then nearest. The top one is spoken only if enough time has passed since the last speech: 0 s for critical, 1.5 s for warning, 3 s for info (MIN_GAP), or if it is more urgent than what was said last. Anything not spoken this cycle is not marked as announced, so it gets its turn on a later cycle instead of being silently lost.

Step 5: grouping and phrasing

For info alerts, objects with the same label at the same clock position are merged: "3 people at 1 o'clock, nearest 4 meters". The phrase is built by nova/phrasing.py:

  • Direction: "ahead" if at 12 o'clock and in the path; otherwise "at N o'clock".
  • Distance: under 1 m to 0.1 m precision; above that, rounded to the nearest half meter, because "2.5 meters" is easier to hear than "2.37 meters" and the estimate is not that precise anyway. Steps use step_length = 0.7 m; feet use 3.28084 ft per meter.
  • Critical phrases start with "Stop.": one word that means "act now" before any detail.
  • "approaching" is added only for people, vehicles and animals.

Step 6: path-clear feedback

Silence is ambiguous: does it mean "nothing there" or "the program crashed"? NOVA therefore:

  • says "Path clear ahead" once an obstacle you were warned about has been out of your path for 3 s (PATH_CLEAR_DEBOUNCE);
  • says "Path clear" as a heartbeat if nothing has been said for 20 s (path_clear_interval) and nothing is in the path.

Worked example: one second in a corridor

  1. t = 0.00 s. A chair appears at 2.2 m, in the path. Hits = 1 → wait (debounce).
  2. t = 0.25 s. Same chair (same track ID), hits = 2 → warning → "Chair ahead, 2 meters".
  3. t = 0.50–2.00 s. Chair still there at the same urgency → silent (already announced).
  4. t = 2.25 s. User walked closer: chair now at 1.1 m → critical, more urgent → immediately "Stop. Chair ahead, 1 meter", interrupting any speech.
  5. t = 8.3 s. Still there, still critical, 6 s passed → one reminder "Stop. Chair ahead, 1 meter". Then quiet.
  6. User steps around it. The chair leaves the corridor; 3 s later → "Path clear ahead".

On-demand answers (nova/scene.py)

  • Space / D, describe_scene(): counts objects seen in the last second (from the tracker, so one missed frame does not mean "nothing here"), groups them by label and position, keeps the 5 nearest groups, then speaks them left to right to match the user's mental picture. Example: "3 objects. Chair ahead, 2 meters. 2 people 1 o'clock, 4 meters."
  • A, describe_path(): answers "is my way clear?" using only objects in the corridor, and if something is close, suggests the side with fewer nearby obstacles: "Chair in your path, 1.5 meters. More room on your right." This is only a hint; the cane or dog has the final say.

10 questions
Why not just announce every detected object?

Because the user would be overwhelmed and could not hear traffic or people. Our reference paper stresses that non-visual senses carry much less information than vision, so information must be filtered. NOVA says at most one phrase per cycle, only what is relevant, and each object only once unless it becomes more urgent.

How did you choose 1.2 m and 2.5 m?

From walking speed and reaction time. At about 1.4 m/s, 1.2 m gives roughly 0.85 s, the minimum to hear "Stop" and halt. 2.5 m gives about 2 s, enough to hear a sentence and steer around. They are engineering defaults, not experimentally optimised values, and are configurable. Tuning them with real users is future work.

Why three urgency levels and not two or five?

Three maps to three different user actions: stop now (critical), adjust your path (warning), just be aware (info). Each level has its own tone, its own speech priority and its own minimum gap. More levels would be hard to tell apart by ear; two would mix "stop now" with "be aware", which is dangerous.

Why is a critical alert not debounced like the others?

The costs are unequal. If a critical detection is a false alarm, the user stops for a moment. If it is real and we wait one more cycle, they may walk into it. For critical, speed wins; for everything else, avoiding false alarms wins.

Why say each object only once? Isn't repetition safer?

Repetition causes alarm fatigue: users start ignoring a device that repeats itself, and eventually turn it off, which is the least safe outcome. Critical objects do get one reminder after 6 s. Anything that becomes more urgent is announced immediately. And the user can ask at any time with Space. The number of critical repeats is configurable.

How do you prevent a warning from being lost if NOVA is busy speaking?

An object is only marked "announced" when it is actually chosen and sent to speech. If a warning waits because of the minimum gap, it stays a candidate and is spoken on a later cycle. There is a test for this: test_unspoken_hazards_are_not_lost.

Why sort by urgency first and distance second?

A chair 1 m ahead in your path (critical) matters more than a person 0.9 m away at your side (warning), even though the person is closer. Within the same urgency, the nearest is the most pressing.

Why does NOVA say "Path clear"? Isn't silence enough?

Silence cannot be told apart from a crash, a frozen camera or a dead battery. The one-time "Path clear ahead" confirms that the obstacle you were warned about is gone, and the 20-second heartbeat proves the system is alive. Both are quiet low-priority messages.

Why round distances to half a meter?

The estimate is not precise to centimeters, so speaking "2.37 meters" would suggest false precision and take longer to say. Half-meter steps are easy to hear and good enough to act on. Under 1 m we use 0.1 m steps, because there small differences matter.

Could you use a machine learning model or an LLM for these decisions?

Not for safety alerts. They must be fast, deterministic and explainable, and must be testable with exact expected outputs (our alert tests check exact phrases). An LLM would add latency and unpredictability. An LLM could be added on the slow path for rich, on-request descriptions, as planned in our roadmap, but never in front of a "Stop".

Speaking to the user: speech and spatial earcons

How NOVA's voice and tones work, in nova/audio.py.

Text-to-speech with pyttsx3

pyttsx3 is a Python library that drives the text-to-speech engine built into the operating system: SAPI5 on Windows (voices such as Microsoft David and Zira), NSSpeechSynthesizer on macOS, eSpeak on Linux. It works fully offline, starts instantly and costs nothing. Settings: voice (--voice Zira), rate in words per minute (default 175, adjustable with +/- between 100 and 300) and volume.

Bug story: "run loop already started"

Re-using one pyttsx3 engine from a background thread on Windows caused the error run loop already started, and speech silently stopped. The fix in Pyttsx3Backend: create a fresh engine for every utterance, all on one dedicated speech thread. Our real-run logs show 111 speech attempts and 0 errors after the fix.

The pending slot: speech priorities

NOVA must never fall behind and read out old news. A normal queue would do exactly that: if five messages pile up, the fifth is spoken after the first four, about a situation that has already changed. NOVA instead uses a single pending slot (PendingSlot):

  • There is at most one message waiting. A new message replaces the waiting one, so the user always hears the latest situation.
  • Except: a waiting message can never be replaced by a less urgent one. A queued "Stop" cannot be pushed out by "Path clear".
  • Priorities: 0 = critical, 1 = normal, 2 = low.
  • A critical message also calls backend.stop(), cutting off the sentence currently being spoken.
  • Quiet mode (M) drops everything except critical and user-requested messages.

Earcons: meaning before words

An earcon is a short abstract sound with a learned meaning, like a notification beep. (The term comes from Blattner, Sumikawa and Greenberg, 1989.) A sentence takes 1–2 s to say; a tone takes 0.1–0.2 s. So NOVA plays a tone first, and the user can start reacting before the words finish.

The tones are synthesised in code (synth_earcon()) as sine waves at 22,050 samples per second, with an 8 ms fade in and out to avoid clicks, and packed into an in-memory WAV file that Windows plays with winsound. Each (kind, direction) combination is cached after the first time.

Stereo panning: direction by ear

The tone is louder in the left or right ear depending on where the object is. The pan value runs from −1 (full left) to +1 (full right) and is the object's angle divided by half the field of view. NOVA uses equal-power panning:

θ = (pan + 1) · π / 4 (0 … π/2) left_gain = cos θ right_gain = sin θ Centre (pan 0): θ = 45°, both gains 0.707. Full right (pan 1): left 0, right 1. Because cos²θ + sin²θ = 1, the total sound power stays constant as the sound moves, so a centred tone does not sound quieter than a side tone. Simple linear panning (0.5 + 0.5) would create a quiet "hole" in the middle.

Panel questions

7 questions
Why pyttsx3 and not a neural voice like edge-tts, Google TTS or Piper?

Speed and reliability first. pyttsx3 uses voices built into the OS, needs no internet, starts speaking immediately and has no cost or rate limits. Cloud neural voices sound nicer but add network latency and fail offline. A local neural voice (Piper) is a good future option and our code is ready for it: speech goes through a backend class, so a new backend can be plugged in without changing anything else.

Why only one waiting message instead of a queue?

A queue reads out the past. If four messages are queued, the user hears about a situation that changed seconds ago. With one slot, newer messages replace older ones, so speech always reflects the present, and the priority rule guarantees an urgent message is never replaced by a less urgent one.

Why play a tone before speaking?

Speed of understanding. A tone takes about 0.1 s and, once learned, its pitch tells you urgency and its left/right balance tells you the side. That is enough to start reacting before the sentence "Stop. Chair at 1 o'clock" is finished. The review paper also highlights spatial sound as an immediately perceivable cue for direction.

What is equal-power panning and why use it?

It sets left gain = cos θ and right gain = sin θ. Because cos² + sin² = 1, the perceived loudness stays the same wherever the sound is placed. With linear panning, a centred sound is only half amplitude in each ear and sounds quieter, which would make objects straight ahead, the most important ones, the least noticeable.

Don't headphones block traffic sounds that blind people rely on?

Yes, closed headphones would be a real safety problem, which the review paper also notes. That is why our sounds are short and sparse, and why we recommend bone-conduction headphones, which sit in front of the ear and leave the ear canal open, while still delivering stereo.

Why generate the tones in code instead of using sound files?

Each tone needs to exist in many stereo positions. Generating them with a few lines of maths (a sine wave, an envelope, two gains) means no asset files, exact control over pitch and duration, and easy changes. Each version is generated once and cached.

What happens on Linux or macOS?

Speech works (pyttsx3 supports them). Earcons use the Windows-only winsound module, so on other systems they are switched off automatically and the rest of NOVA runs normally. Keyboard input falls back to typing a key followed by Enter.

Staying trustworthy

Camera-health checks, traffic-light reading and camera recovery: the features that stop "no alert" from silently meaning "I can't see".

Camera health (nova/environment.py)

The most dangerous failure of a vision aid is not a wrong alert; it is silence when it cannot see. In the dark, with a finger over the lens, or with a frozen camera, YOLO detects nothing, and "nothing detected" sounds exactly like "path clear". So every detector cycle, NOVA checks the image itself on a 160×120 grayscale copy:

ProblemMeasurementThresholdSpoken message

A problem must be seen on 3 consecutive checks before it is announced (so a hand passing in front of the lens does not trigger it), and a lasting problem is repeated every 30 s. Traffic-light colour (nova/traffic_light.py)

When YOLO finds a traffic light, NOVA reads its colour with plain image processing, no extra network:

  1. Crop the YOLO box and convert it to HSV.
  2. Keep only "lit" pixels: saturation ≥ 90 (strongly coloured) and value ≥ 130 (bright). This throws away the dark housing and the unlit lamps.
  3. Count lit pixels whose hue falls in each range: red 0–10 or 165–179, yellow 15–35, green 40–95.
  4. The colour with the most pixels wins, but only if they are at least 3% of the box; otherwise "unknown".
  5. The nearest light's colour must be the same on 2 consecutive checks before NOVA speaks: "Traffic light at 12 o'clock appears red", later "…changed to green".
Why the wording says "appears"

Sun glare, viewing angle, LED flicker and the difference between pedestrian and vehicle lights can all fool a colour rule. NOVA says "appears red" on purpose, and the README tells users never to cross based on NOVA alone. Being honest about uncertainty is part of safe design.

Camera recovery and startup (nova/capture.py)

  • Auto-select: scans camera indices 0–4 and prefers index 0 (usually the external USB webcam when one is plugged in). --camera N skips the scan.
  • DirectShow on Windows (cv2.CAP_DSHOW) avoids multi-second timeouts when probing empty camera indices.
  • Reconnect: if a frame read fails, NOVA says "Camera lost. Reconnecting." and retries every second for up to 30 s, then says "Camera reconnected." or "Camera unavailable."
  • Spoken lifecycle: "Starting NOVA." → model warm-up → "NOVA ready…" → on exit "Shutting down." A blind user always knows the program's state.
  • Other inputs: --input clip.mp4 reads a video; --input folder/ reads images in sorted order. Recorded input is analysed frame by frame and ends with "End of recording."

Panel questions

6 questions
Why are camera-health checks needed at all?

Because a detector that cannot see produces no detections, and no detections sounds like a clear path. That is the most dangerous failure an obstacle assistant can have. The checks turn invisible failures into spoken warnings, so the user knows when not to trust NOVA.

How did you pick the thresholds (35, 12, 15, 0.5)?

Empirically, from test images and webcam footage, choosing values that separate normal frames clearly from the failure cases. Our measurements show wide margins (for example sharpness 1499 normal vs 5.3 blurred, threshold 15). They are not tuned on a large dataset, and very low-texture scenes (a plain white wall close up) can trigger "blurry" or "blocked"; that is acceptable, because NOVA is then honestly saying it cannot see much.

Why require 3 consecutive checks before announcing?

To avoid false alarms from momentary events: a hand waving in front of the lens, a quick head turn causing motion blur, a single dark frame from auto-exposure. Three checks is about 0.75 s at 4 Hz, still quick.

Why not train a CNN to classify traffic-light colour?

YOLO already finds the light; inside its box the problem is simple: which lamp is glowing. A few HSV rules solve that in microseconds with no training data. A CNN might be more robust to glare and angles, but would need a labelled dataset. Given the safety disclaimer that NOVA's reading is only an aid, the simple method is proportionate.

Why only report the nearest traffic light?

At a junction several lights may be visible, often for different directions. Reporting all of them would be confusing. The nearest is the most likely to be the one in front of the user. Even so, NOVA cannot tell which road a light controls, which is why it is an aid only.

What if the camera is unplugged during use?

NOVA speaks "Camera lost. Reconnecting.", retries every second for 30 s, and either confirms "Camera reconnected." or says "Camera unavailable." and stops. The user is never left in silence. An early version crashed silently in this situation.

Panel questions

5 questions
Why keyboard controls? A blind person walking can't use a keyboard.

Fair point. The keyboard is the prototype's input method because it runs on a laptop. The commands are designed so they can be mapped to any input: a phone's volume buttons, a small Bluetooth clicker, or voice. Voice commands with an offline speech recogniser (Vosk) are the first item on our roadmap, mapping phrases like "what's around me?" to the same command handlers.

Why read keys from the terminal instead of the OpenCV window?

OpenCV only receives keys when its window is focused, which a blind user cannot see or click. Reading the terminal works with a screen reader, with no window, and in headless mode. We support both so sighted helpers can use the window too.

Why a configuration file instead of just command-line flags?

Accessibility. Typing --units steps --rate 200 --voice Zira --verbosity minimal every time is tedious for anyone and harder with a screen reader. A file remembers personal preferences, and key-adjusted preferences save themselves.

Why "steps" as a unit?

Many blind users think in steps rather than meters, because steps are what they physically count. The step length (default 0.7 m) is configurable to match the person.

What does pause actually reset, and why?

Pausing clears the tracker and the alert memory. While paused, the world changes and old tracks become meaningless; on resume, NOVA starts fresh so it announces the current surroundings instead of comparing with a stale past.

The visual layer: HUD, radar, captions and recording

What a sighted helper, a low-vision user or an audience sees. None of it is needed by a blind user, but it makes NOVA explainable.

The HUD overlay (nova/hud.py)

  • Boxes coloured by urgency: red critical, orange warning, green info, grey ignored. Each is labelled with name, distance and clock hour; >> marks an approaching object, and traffic lights show their colour.
  • Clock sector lines at ±15° and ±45° (true angles via x_of_angle) with hour numbers along the bottom.
  • Walking corridor drawn as two dashed lines where the 1 m corridor would be at the warning distance (2.5 m).
  • An arrow from the bottom centre to the nearest critical or warning object.
  • Status bar: FPS, verbosity, units, speech rate and flags such as PAUSED, QUIET or DARK.

The bird's-eye radar (nova/radar.py)

A top-down map placed beside the camera image. The user is the white triangle at the bottom; up the page is forward. It draws the camera's field-of-view wedge, a ring every meter, and the walking corridor shaded red where an object would be critical (0–1.2 m), orange for warning (1.2–2.5 m) and teal beyond. Each object is drawn at its real distance and sideways offset as a bar as wide as the object (from its left and right edges in meters), with its track ID. Approaching objects get an arrow showing where they will be in one second (closing_speed × 1 s).

The radar is the clearest way to explain NOVA's reasoning to an audience: you can literally see that a bar overlaps the corridor (so it is "in path") or not.

Captions (nova/captions.py)

Every message that is actually accepted for speech appears as a subtitle on the image, coloured by urgency. Each caption stays for 2.5–8 s depending on its length (0.06 s per character), at most two are visible, and long text wraps to the frame width. For recorded input the caption clock follows video time (frame number ÷ frame rate), so subtitles line up with the video even though processing ran faster or slower than real time.

Recording (nova/recorder.py)

--record demo.mp4 saves the full composed view (camera + HUD + captions + radar). A live camera delivers frames irregularly, but a video file needs a fixed frame rate. The recorder therefore places frames on a fixed 15 fps timeline by wall-clock time: if frames arrive slowly, the last one is repeated to fill the gap; if they arrive faster, extras are skipped. The result plays back at real speed. Recorded input keeps its own frame rate.

Panel questions

4 questions
Why build visual features for a tool meant for blind users?

Three audiences benefit: sighted helpers and O&M trainers who need to see what NOVA is reacting to, low-vision users who can see large colours, and developers and examiners who need to verify the logic. The visual layer is optional (--headless, --no-radar, --no-captions) and never affects the alerts.

What does the radar show that the camera view does not?

Metric space. In the camera image, a far car and a near chair can look the same size, and the corridor is a perspective shape. On the radar, everything is in meters from above, so distance, sideways offset, corridor overlap and approach are directly visible. It is a visualisation of exactly the numbers the alert policy uses.

Why do live recordings duplicate or drop frames?

Video files assume a constant frame rate, but a live loop's speed varies (detection cycles, CPU load). Writing frames as they come would make the video play too fast or too slow. Mapping frames onto a fixed 15 fps clock by their timestamps keeps playback at real-world speed.

Why only caption messages that were actually accepted for speech?

So captions are a truthful transcript of what the user heard. A message rejected by quiet mode or by the speech priority rule is not shown, otherwise the recording would claim NOVA said something it did not.

6 questions
Walk us through what happens when you run python assistive_system.py.

It parses options and builds Settings (defaults, then nova_config.json, then flags). NovaApp creates the metrics logger, speech, earcons, keyboard listener, alert policy and tracker. run() says "Starting NOVA", loads and warms up YOLO, opens the camera, starts the keyboard thread and says "NOVA ready". Why is Detection a dataclass?

It is a plain record with named fields, which makes code readable (d.distance instead of d[4]), gives a free constructor and readable printing, and makes building test objects easy (tests/helpers.py creates fake detections in one line).

Why are heavy imports (torch, ultralytics, pyttsx3) done lazily?

So that commands like --help or --save-config start instantly, and so the test suite runs without a GPU, a model file or an audio device. YOLO is imported only inside Detector.__init__, pyttsx3 only inside Pyttsx3Backend.

What are the dependencies and why so few?

Three: opencv-python (camera, image processing, drawing), ultralytics (YOLOv8, which brings PyTorch) and pyttsx3 (speech). Why isn't the model file in the repository?

It is a 6.5 MB binary that git handles poorly, and Ultralytics downloads the official weights automatically when YOLO("yolov8n.pt") does not find the file. The .gitignore excludes *.pt on purpose.

15.2 A failure case worth understanding

What went wrong, step by step:

  1. The man leans forward and the box also includes an outstretched arm, so the box is very wide: 667 px.
  2. Width-based distance assumes a person is 0.45 m wide: 739.6 × 0.45 / 667 = 0.50 m.
  3. Height-based distance assumes a full 1.70 m body, but only the upper body is visible: 739.6 × 1.70 / 343 = 3.66 m.
  4. NOVA takes the smaller, 0.5 m, and the box is centred in the corridor, so the result is critical.

Both estimates are wrong in opposite directions, because both size assumptions are broken (wide pose, half-visible body). The "pick the closer" rule turns this uncertainty into a false alarm instead of a missed obstacle, which is the safer of the two errors, but it is still an error. Fixes: use a monocular depth network instead of size priors, use YOLO pose keypoints to measure shoulder width or head size (more stable than box size), or fuse several cues.

What does your latency number actually measure?

The time from when an object's track was first seen to when NOVA spoke about it. For objects that were dangerous from the start, that is the true reaction time: 0.06–0.5 s, median about 0.14 s in the real-model video run. For objects that were seen far away and only became dangerous later, it includes the time the user took to approach them, which is why the 95th percentile is 8.5 s. Why report median instead of mean?

The data is skewed by rare extreme values: one 11.4 s detection, and latencies of many seconds caused by the metric definition. The mean (0.275 s detection, 2.1 s latency) is pulled up by those few values; the median (72 ms, 0.55 s) describes a typical cycle. We report both, plus the 95th percentile.

How accurate are the distances in your results?

We have not measured that yet against a tape measure, and we will not claim a number we have not measured. The real-image results show the method working sensibly in one case and failing in another (a leaning person with an outstretched arm estimated at 0.5 m). The planned protocol is in section 15.5.

Your sample images are stock photos. Why not your own test images?

Our own live sessions are in the field logs. For the notebook figures we used the standard Ultralytics sample images so that anyone can reproduce them exactly. Our own recorded walkthrough with --record is the next step for the presentation demo.

Why is the FPS sometimes as low as 1?

Display and detection share one loop. During startup, model loading or a slow detection cycle, one display frame waits, so that second's FPS sample drops. Session 1 was blocked for its whole 26 s. Alerts are not affected by preview FPS. Moving detection to its own thread would keep the preview smooth even during slow cycles.

How do you know the TTS fix worked?

The metrics logger records an "attempt" before each phrase and a "success" or "error" after it. Across our field sessions: 111 attempts, 111 successes, 0 errors. Before the fix, the error "run loop already started" made speech stop silently.

What is the most important weakness your results reveal?

Distance from object size. It breaks when the size assumption breaks: unusual poses, partly visible bodies, or large objects filling the frame. In our results this gave false "Stop" alerts (Figures 15.2 and 15.3c). The failure is in the safe direction (too close, not too far), but too many false alarms erode trust. Monocular depth estimation is our planned fix.

What is the domain gap you mention?

The difference between the data a model was trained on and the data it meets in use. COCO images are mostly well-composed photos; NOVA sees close-up, moving, sometimes blurred views of rooms and streets from chest height. Accuracy drops on such images, and we saw odd labels like "refrigerator" for large flat indoor surfaces. Fine-tuning on navigation footage reduces the gap.

Ethics and responsibility

  • Over-trust is the main risk. A user might trust NOVA more than it deserves. Hence the safety notice, the cautious wording ("appears red"), the camera-health warnings, and positioning NOVA as a complement to the cane.
  • Privacy. The camera films bystanders. NOVA processes everything locally, keeps no images by default (only when --record is explicitly used) and sends nothing over the internet.
  • Bias in the detector. Detection quality can vary across environments, lighting and how people look. COCO is mostly Western, well-lit photos. Testing in the user's real environment matters.
  • Accessibility of the tool itself. Every function works without sight: spoken startup and shutdown, terminal keys, spoken help, saved preferences.

Roadmap (from next_steps.md)

  1. Voice commands with an offline wake word and recogniser (Vosk), mapped onto the existing command handlers.
  2. Stairs, kerbs, poles and doors, via fine-tuning or monocular depth (Depth Anything V2, MiDaS).
  3. OCR for signs (EasyOCR or PaddleOCR) on request: room numbers, shop names.
  4. Conversational descriptions with a small local language model, strictly on the slow path, never delaying safety alerts.
  5. Neural TTS (Piper offline, or edge-tts) as a pluggable speech backend.
  6. Evaluation: taped distance accuracy, ablation of tracking and debounce, and a user study.

Panel questions

5 questions
If you had three more months, what would you do first?

First, measure: a taped distance-accuracy test and an ablation study, so every claim has a number. Second, add monocular depth estimation, which addresses the two biggest weaknesses at once: unlabelled obstacles (walls, poles, drop-offs) and size-based distance errors. Third, a small user study with visually impaired participants to tune thresholds and wording.

How would monocular depth estimation fit into the current design?

A depth network such as Depth Anything V2 Small outputs a depth value per pixel. We would (1) take the median depth inside each YOLO box as a second distance estimate and combine it with the size estimate, and (2) look for anything close inside the corridor region of the depth map, even with no YOLO label, which catches walls and poles. Because it is slower than YOLO, it would run less often, and drop-off detection would need a scale correction since these models give relative depth.

Could NOVA run on a phone?

The pipeline is light enough: YOLOv8n can be exported to TFLite or Core ML and runs in real time on modern phones, and everything else is simple maths. The work is porting the Python code (or wrapping it), using the phone's TTS, and replacing keyboard input with buttons or voice. A phone also adds a gyroscope for tilt and ego-motion.

Who is liable if a user gets hurt?

That is exactly why NOVA is presented as a research aid and not a medical or safety device. A real product would need regulatory approval, extensive testing and clear labelling. Our design choices (complement to the cane, cautious wording, warnings when it cannot see) are meant to prevent over-reliance.

Why not use an LLM or vision-language model to describe everything?

They are too slow and unpredictable for safety alerts: seconds of latency, possible hallucinations and no guaranteed wording. They are excellent for rich answers on request ("What does this room look like?"), which is how our roadmap uses them: only on the slow path, after the user asks, never in front of a "Stop".

Quick answers

21 questions
What is the input and output of your system?

Input: video frames from a single RGB camera. Output: short spoken phrases and stereo tones, plus an optional visual HUD and recording.

Which part is computer vision and which is not?

CV: object detection (YOLOv8), pinhole geometry for distance and direction, IoU tracking, HSV colour classification, and image-quality analysis (brightness, contrast, Laplacian sharpness, frame differencing). Not CV: the alert rules, speech and controls.

What is the formula for distance?

Z = f · H_real / h_pixels, with f = (W/2) / tan(HFOV/2).

What is the focal length in pixels for a 640-wide, 60° camera?

320 / tan 30° ≈ 554 pixels.

How many degrees is one clock hour?

30°, because 360° / 12 hours.

What is IoU?

Overlap area divided by combined area of two boxes; 1 means identical, 0 means no overlap.

What is NMS?

Non-maximum suppression: keeps the most confident of several overlapping boxes for the same object and removes the rest.

What does mAP mean?

Mean Average Precision: the area under the precision–recall curve, averaged over classes (and over IoU thresholds for mAP@50-95).

What is time-to-contact?

Distance divided by closing speed: how many seconds until the object reaches you if nothing changes.

What are your three urgency levels?

Critical (stop now, interrupts speech), warning (adjust path), info (awareness).

What is the critical distance?

1.2 m inside the 1 m wide walking corridor, or any approaching object with time-to-contact under 2 s.

How often does YOLO run?

Every 0.25 s on a live camera (up to 4 times a second); every frame on recorded video.

How fast is detection?

About 44 ms median in our CPU benchmark and 72 ms median in the field logs.

What is an earcon?

A short abstract tone with a learned meaning; ours encode urgency by pitch and pattern, and direction by stereo balance.

What is the exponential moving average used for?

Smoothing each object's distance over time, with α = 0.5.

Why is the system offline?

Predictable low latency, works without signal, and privacy, since no images leave the device.

How many classes does NOVA detect?

26 of COCO's 80, those relevant to walking and with fairly consistent real sizes.

How many tests do you have?

55 test functions, 63 test cases, all passing, with no camera, speaker or model needed.

What was the hardest bug?

Speech silently stopping because of the pyttsx3 "run loop already started" error on Windows. Fixed by one dedicated speech thread creating a fresh engine per phrase; the logs then showed 111 of 111 phrases spoken.

What would you change if starting again?

Build the evaluation set-up (taped distances, recorded test clips with expected alerts) on day one, and plan for depth estimation from the start instead of relying only on object sizes.
